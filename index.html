The two-year journey at a glance

St Peter's students sit the IB's November examination session at the end of Year 13. Your teachers set the internal deadlines, so use this as the shape of the journey, then add your actual dates to the planner.

Year 12 · Terms 1–2

Settle into six subjects. Start CAS experiences and your CAS portfolio. TOK classes begin with the core theme, Knowledge and the Knower.

Year 12 · Term 2–3

Choose your EE subject and supervisor. First EE reflection session. Start planning a CAS project. First CAS interview.

Year 12 · Terms 3–4

TOK exhibition. EE research and a full plan. Second EE reflection session. Internal assessments start appearing in subjects.

Year 13 · Term 1

EE full draft due mid-term. Final EE due at the end of Term 1, followed by the viva voce (third reflection session). IA deadlines start to cluster, so plan early.

Year 13 · Terms 2–3

TOK essay on a prescribed title. Finish remaining IAs, CAS evidence and the final CAS interview. Mock exams. Revision becomes a daily habit.

Year 13 · Term 4

November examination session. Results arrive around 17 December.

The Diploma

What the IB Diploma is

The International Baccalaureate (IB) is a non-profit foundation set up in Geneva in 1968. Its Diploma Programme (DP) is a two-year course for 16–19 year olds, recognised by universities around the world.

The idea behind it

The DP aims for breadth and depth: you keep studying languages, a science, maths, the humanities and (often) the arts, while going deep in three or four subjects at Higher Level. The core asks you to think about knowledge itself (TOK), research independently (EE) and grow as a person outside the classroom (CAS).

The IB learner profile

Ten attributes the whole programme is built to develop. You will see them in CAS reflections, TOK discussions and your EE reflection.

How the Diploma is structured

You take one subject from each of groups 1–5, plus either a group 6 arts subject or a second subject from groups 1–4. At least three (and up to four) are at Higher Level (about 240 teaching hours each); the rest are Standard Level (about 150 hours).

1 · Studies in language & literaturee.g. English A
2 · Language acquisitione.g. French B, Spanish ab initio
3 · Individuals & societiese.g. History, Economics
4 · Sciencese.g. Biology, Chemistry, Physics
5 · MathematicsAnalysis & approaches, or Applications & interpretation
6 · The artse.g. Visual Arts, Music (or a second 1–4 subject)
The core, studied by everyone

Theory of Knowledge

About 100 hours. Assessed by an exhibition (internal) and a 1,600-word essay (external).

Extended Essay

An independent 4,000-word research essay, around 40 hours of work, with three supervisor reflection sessions.

Creativity, Activity, Service

About 18 months of experiences and at least one CAS project, evidenced in a portfolio. Not graded, but required.

How points work

Each of your six subjects is graded 1 to 7, so subjects give up to 42 points. TOK and the EE are each graded A to E, and together they earn up to 3 core points from the matrix below. The maximum is 45.

To be awarded the Diploma

From the IB's published passing criteria. All must be true.

  • CAS requirements are met.
  • At least 24 points in total.
  • A grade in every subject, TOK and the EE (no missing work).
  • No subject below a 2.
  • No more than two grade 2s.
  • No more than three grades of 3 or below.
  • At least 12 points from HL subjects (if you take four HL, your best three count).
  • At least 9 points from SL subjects (5 points if you take only two SL subjects).
  • No E in TOK or the EE, and no academic misconduct finding.

Creativity · Activity · Service

CAS: learning that happens outside the classroom

CAS runs for at least 18 months. You don't count hours; you show growth. Your portfolio holds the evidence and reflections that show you have met all seven learning outcomes.

Creativity

Exploring and extending ideas that lead to an original or interpretive product or performance.

Learning an instrument, designing a set for the school production, starting a podcast, carving, photography, choreographing a haka performance.

Activity

Physical exertion that contributes to a healthy lifestyle.

Rowing at Lake Karāpiro, a new sport, training for a half marathon, coaching junior netball, tramping in the Kaimai Range.

Service

Collaborative, reciprocal engagement with the community in response to an authentic need.

Riparian planting on the Waikato River, tutoring juniors, volunteering at a food bank, helping at a retirement village.

What you must do

  • Take part in a balance of creativity, activity and service experiences across at least 18 months.
  • Complete at least one CAS project: collaborative, lasting at least a month, and challenging you to show initiative.
  • Keep a CAS portfolio with evidence and reflections.
  • Show evidence for all seven learning outcomes.
  • Attend three interviews with your CAS coordinator or adviser (normally start, middle and end).

The five CAS stages

1Investigation: what are your interests, skills and a real need?
2Preparation: plan roles, timeline and resources.
3Action: do it, adapt as you go.
4Reflection: think about what happened and what it means.
5Demonstration: show what you did and learned.

What a strong reflection looks like

These examples were written for this site to show the difference. They are not real student work.

Thin
Today I went to rowing training. It was really hard and cold but fun. We did 3 x 2 km pieces. I think I am getting better. I am looking forward to the regatta.

It describes the session but there's no learning, no "why", no link to an outcome, and no next step. It reads like a diary entry.

Strong · LO4 Commitment and perseverance
Our 5 am session at Karāpiro was 3 × 2 km pieces in 4°C. On the second piece my split drifted out by four seconds and I wanted to stop. I noticed that I give up mentally before I give up physically, so on the third piece I focused on counting ten strokes at a time. My split held. This is the first time I've stuck at something when it was boring and hard, not just when it was going well, which is what commitment means to me now. Before the regatta I'll ask our coach to film my catch so I have something concrete to work on during the dark winter sessions.

Specific detail, an honest insight, a clear link to an outcome in the student's own words, and a next step.

Thin
I helped at the food bank. I learned that lots of people need help and it made me feel grateful for what I have. It was a good experience and showed global engagement.

Names an outcome but doesn't show it. "Lots of people need help" is general, not something this student found out.

Strong · LO6 Global engagement, LO7 Ethics
Sorting donations, I saw that we had 40 tins of beans but almost no nappies or sanitary products. The manager said those are what families ask for most. I'd assumed food was the whole problem. Food insecurity is part of a wider pattern of cost-of-living pressure, which is happening here and around the world. I'm uncomfortable that I'd been "helping" based on what was easy to give. For our house drive next term I want to ask the food bank for a needs list first and share it with the year group.

Starts from a real observation, connects a local need to a global issue, and questions the student's own assumptions.

Quick test before you post a reflection: could someone tell from this what you learned, not just what you did? Is there one sentence only you could have written? Have you said what's next?

Extended Essay

The Extended Essay

A 4,000-word independent research essay on a question you choose, supervised by a teacher. It is your chance to go deep on something you care about, and universities often ask about it.

Which criteria apply to you? The IB introduced a new EE model with a new guide in 2025. It applies to every student sitting exams from the May 2027 session onwards, which includes St Peter's students sitting in November 2027 and later. The old model (criteria A–E out of 34) ended with the November 2026 session. Everything on this page is the new model.
Length

4,000 words

Maximum. Contents page, tables, figures, citations, bibliography and the RPF aren't counted.

Time

About 40 hours

Spread over roughly a year. Start early, finish calmly.

Pathways

Subject-focused or interdisciplinary

Interdisciplinary essays replace the old World Studies EE.

Reflection

3 sessions + RPF

Three formal sessions with your supervisor, the last being the viva voce, then a 500-word statement on the Reflection and Progress Form.

Questions you might ask

Is my research question too broad?

If you could write a book on it, yes. A good question names a specific case, text, period, place or variable. "How did the 1981 Springbok tour change New Zealand?" is too broad. "To what extent did coverage in the Waikato Times shape local opinion about the Hamilton match of the 1981 Springbok tour?" can be answered in 4,000 words.

Can I use a topic from my IA?

You can't submit the same work twice. The EE must be a different investigation, and you should check overlap with your supervisor early.

How much help can my supervisor give?

They can discuss ideas, suggest sources, ask questions and read and comment on one complete draft. They cannot edit or rewrite it for you.

Do I need primary research?

It depends on the subject. Sciences usually need data (from experiments or databases), history needs primary sources, English needs close reading of texts. Ask what counts as good evidence in your subject.

What happens in the viva voce?

A 20–30 minute conversation with your supervisor after you submit. It checks the work is yours and helps you reflect on what you learned. It is not an exam on your argument.

Can I use AI tools?

Follow the IB's academic integrity policy and the school's rules. Any AI-generated material you use must be credited and cited like any other source, and your argument and writing must be your own. If in doubt, ask before you use it.

Theory of Knowledge

How do we know what we claim to know?

TOK is about knowing, not facts. You look at how knowledge is built, tested, shared and challenged in different areas, and what that means for you as a knower.

How the course is organised

Core theme: Knowledge and the Knower.

Optional themes (you study two): Knowledge and Technology, Knowledge and Language, Knowledge and Politics, Knowledge and Religion, Knowledge and Indigenous Societies.

Areas of knowledge (AOKs): History, the Human Sciences, the Natural Sciences, Mathematics, the Arts.

The knowledge framework

Four lenses you can apply to any theme or AOK. Use them to generate knowledge questions.

ScopeWhat is this area about? What problems does it try to solve?
PerspectivesWho produces this knowledge? Whose views count?
Methods & toolsHow is knowledge produced and checked here?
EthicsWhat responsibilities come with knowing this?
Internal · one third of TOK

The exhibition

Three objects linked to one of the IB's 35 "IA prompts", with a commentary of up to 950 words. Based on the core theme or one optional theme. Marked out of 10 by your teacher and moderated by the IB.

External · two thirds of TOK

The essay

A 1,600-word essay on one of six prescribed titles released by the IB for your exam session. Marked out of 10 by an IB examiner.

Areas of knowledge

For each AOK: how knowledge gets made, how it gets shared and tested, and real examples to think with.

The exhibition

Example prompts with object ideas, many from Aotearoa. A strong object is specific (this one, made by this person, on this date), linked to you or your world, and lets you say something about knowledge. Your teacher has the full list of 35 prompts.

What examiners are looking for

One holistic question: can the exhibition show how TOK manifests in the world around us? High-scoring exhibitions:

  • Identify three specific, real objects and explain the real-world context of each.
  • Make clear, explicit links between each object and the chosen prompt, every time.
  • Give reasons for including each object, supported by evidence and specific references to the prompt.
  • Avoid repetition: each object adds something new to the argument.
Weak

Prompt: What counts as good evidence for a claim?

My object is a photo of a newspaper. Newspapers are sources of evidence. Some newspapers are biased and some are not, so evidence can be good or bad. This links to the prompt because it is about evidence.

Generic object (which paper? when?), restates the prompt, no argument.

Strong

Same prompt, written for this site

My second object is the Waikato Times front page reporting the cancelled Springbok match at Rugby Park, Hamilton, in July 1981. Two readers in my own family remember that day completely differently, yet both point to this page as proof. This suggests a source becomes "good evidence" only once someone decides what question it answers. Unlike my first object, a lab notebook, the evidence here cannot be repeated, so its weight depends on corroboration, not replication.

Specific object, personal context, explicit link to the prompt, and it builds on object 1.

The essay

UnpackDefine the key terms in the title and say what you take the question to be asking.
ClaimA point in response to the title, from one AOK, with a specific real example.
CounterclaimA limitation or opposing view, with its own example.
RepeatDo the same in a second AOK (most titles ask for two).
EvaluateWeigh the claims. What are the implications? Where does it depend on perspective?
ConcludeA clear, qualified answer to the title.
Aim for about 1,550 words. Use specific examples from your subjects, CAS, the EE and your life in New Zealand rather than textbook clichés.

What examiners are looking for

One holistic question: does the student provide a clear, coherent and critical exploration of the essay title? High-scoring essays:

  • Stay focused on the title the whole way through, linking back to it explicitly.
  • Support claims with specific, well-chosen examples.
  • Consider different points of view and evaluate them, rather than listing them.
  • Explore the implications of the argument.
  • Are clearly organised and use TOK concepts accurately.
Weak
In the natural sciences, knowledge is very reliable because scientists use the scientific method. For example, Newton discovered gravity. This shows that science gives us certain knowledge.

Overclaims ("certain"), cliché example with no detail, no counterclaim.

Strong · written for this site
Consensus in the natural sciences is built to be revised. When I tested the effect of temperature on catalase in Year 12 Biology, my class's results only became "knowledge" once we pooled them and the outlier groups found their errors. That suggests reliability comes from a community checking itself, not from any single experiment. Yet this same process can delay acceptance of correct ideas: Barry Marshall's claim that bacteria cause stomach ulcers was rejected for years until he drank a culture of the bacteria himself.

Personal and specific example, a claim and a counterclaim, and an evaluation of the method.

Plan a week you can actually stick to

Five quick steps. Add what's fixed, then what needs doing, and the planner fits your tasks into the free half-hours before school and after school up to 10 pm. It opens with an example week; clear it to start your own.

1 · Your school week

Import your timetable as a calendar file (.ics) if you can export one from your school calendar or timetable app. Otherwise just set your school hours below.

2 · Recurring commitments

Add anything fixed this week: trainings, games, music or tutoring lessons, appointments, a part-time job, and family or wellbeing time. Wellbeing time counts. Sleep, dinner with whānau and downtime protect your study.

3 · To-do tasks with a deadline

Set homework and the very next step on any IA, EE or TOK task. "Work on EE" isn't a next step; "Write 300 words of section 2" is.

4 · Specific study

Turn "study biology" into something you'd know you'd finished. Pick an action, how much, and the topic.
